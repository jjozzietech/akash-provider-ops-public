## // what this is

First of a monthly series. One report per month covering what the provider ran,
what shipped publicly, and what broke. Published on the last Thursday window of
the month.

Two reasons for doing this in public. First, tenants deciding where to deploy have
almost nothing to go on beyond on-chain attributes and a price — a provider that
publishes its own operational record is easier to evaluate than one that doesn't.
Second, it forces a monthly reckoning with the things that are quietly not working.

No marketing. Specific numbers or nothing.

## // what shipped

**Two upstream contributions merged into `cosmos/chain-registry`.**

Public Kava endpoints registered — RPC and REST in one PR, gRPC on a dedicated
port in a follow-up. The split wasn't planned: the gRPC entry was removed during
review because the path-prefix routing the registry expects doesn't work for gRPC,
where the HTTP/2 `:path` header carries the method name rather than a route. Both
landed on the same day once a maintainer picked them up.

**Certificate infrastructure moved to automated renewal.**

Both the provider certificate and the wildcard ingress certificate now renew via
`acme.sh` on a cron, with a file-watcher reload path that avoids provider restarts.
The built-in ACME client is disabled in favour of this. The failure mode that
prompted the change: chart defaults silently reassert themselves on every Helm
upgrade unless explicitly overridden, which is now a documented pre-flight check
rather than something to remember.

**Public repos.**

- `akash-provider-ops-public` — sanitized operational scripts and notes.
  9 commits, monitoring and anti-mining subdirectories.
- `akash-miner-blacklist` — community blacklist of tenants running mining
  workloads in violation of deployment terms. 37 entries, MIT licensed,
  with documented evidence standards for contributions.

Anti-mining enforcement ran clean this month: no active miner workloads detected
at month end, last eviction Aug 7.

**On-chain provider contact updated.**

The provider's on-chain contact moved from a personal address to a role address on
the operator domain. Abuse and security contacts are handled off-chain via standard
role aliases per RFC 2142, since no on-chain convention exists for them. Minor
change, but a provider reachable at a role address rather than someone's personal
mail is a provider that survives its operator changing email.

**Written up in longer form.**

The wildcard certificate renewal work is covered in detail here, including the
DNS-01 CNAME delegation approach and the failure modes that made it necessary:
[Wildcard cert renewal with DNS alias](https://jjozzietech.com.au/wildcard-cert-renewal-dns-alias/)

## // what broke

**Low GPU bid rate — investigated, still open.**

The GPU node is healthy, idle, and advertising on-chain. Open GPU orders exist in
the market. The provider bids on very few of them.

The obvious hypothesis was a broken bid engine — a config value reverted to a chart
default after a Helm upgrade, or a pricing script not being invoked. That was
investigated and ruled out: the bid engine evaluates orders and behaves as
configured. Declining an order is the correct outcome when an order's requirements
don't match what the provider offers, and most of what it sees genuinely doesn't
match.

What's left is harder to fix than a config bug. Orders carry filters — region,
audit status, specific hardware — and au-syd is a small market to begin with.
Compounding that, the provider's audit attestation was removed on Aug 18 as part of
an auditor consolidating their portfolio toward North America and newer hardware.
That wasn't a compliance action and it wasn't specific to this provider, but the
effect is the same either way: any order filtering on audited providers no longer
matches, and a meaningful share of GPU demand filters on exactly that.

So the honest position is that this is unresolved, and the biggest single variable
is outside the provider's control. Re-audit is the obvious lever and has been
raised with the auditor; both major auditors are currently dormant on-chain, so
waiting is the realistic strategy rather than escalating. The other levers are
price and simply waiting for the market to grow.

GPU fleet expansion stays on hold. Adding capacity to a provider that can't bid on
the orders it wants buys nothing until the audit question resolves.

Will report where this lands next month.

**Rack migration — completed, zero loss.**

Nine nodes physically relocated to new rack space on Aug 20. No data loss, no lease
loss, no tenant-visible interruption. One node needed on-site recovery from a
faulty power cable and another had a slot placement corrected during the move,
neither of which reached tenants.

Filed under "what broke" rather than "what shipped" deliberately: a physical
migration of live infrastructure is the kind of change where the interesting part
is what nearly went wrong. Both faults were hardware-level and caught during the
move because the sequencing left room to catch them — nodes drained and verified
healthy one at a time rather than in parallel.

**10G NIC upgrade rejected after investigation.**

Planned a network upgrade, measured first, cancelled it. Per-node utilisation came
in under 1 Mbps with zero interface drops since boot. The current uplink is 80 Mbps
up; the planned replacement is 500 Mbps up. A single existing 1G NIC is already
more than twice the future WAN ceiling, and Akash neither bills nor bids on
bandwidth.

Hardware investigation found only five of nine candidate nodes had clean PCIe
expansion paths anyway. The upgrade would have cost money and downtime to solve a
problem that measurement showed didn't exist. Revisit trigger is documented and
specific: only if persistent storage becomes a real revenue line, and the better
answer then is moving storage off the small-form-factor nodes entirely rather than
adding NICs to them.

**On #182.** Two validator-operations tools submitted to the Cosmos ecosystem list.
Blocked for 45 days on something worth naming, because it will affect anyone
submitting a first contribution to these repos: the required lint check has never
run. It sits at *expected — waiting for status to be reported* rather than
pass or fail, because GitHub Actions holds workflow runs from first-time
contributors until a maintainer approves them. No maintainer has looked, so the
check cannot report, so the PR cannot merge. A bot flagged the same
author-allowlist condition on all three of the above; in `chain-registry` a
maintainer overrode it within a day, and in `awesome-cosmos` nobody is watching.

The bot is not the blocker. Maintainer attention is. Worth knowing before you
spend an evening on a first PR to an unfamiliar repo.
